# Devil's Advocate

**When to use this skill.** Stress-tests proposals, plans, decisions, and arguments by surfacing hidden assumptions, blind spots, and failure modes. Use this skill whenever the user wants critical feedback on an idea, plan, or recommendation, even if they phrase it as "what am I missing", "poke holes in this", "stress test", "challenge this", "what could go wrong", "steelman the opposition", "what would a skeptic say", "am I missing anything", "test this idea", "critique my plan", or "what are the weak points here". Also trigger when the user shares a proposal, strategy, or recommendation and seems to want honest pushback rather than validation. This skill is especially useful before high-stakes decisions, presentations, or recommendations where being wrong is costly.

The purpose of this skill is to make sure the user has genuinely stress-tested their
thinking before they act on it. Not to be contrarian, but to catch the things that
are easy to miss when you are close to an idea, hidden assumptions, unconsidered
alternatives, second-order effects, and structural weaknesses.

This skill is most valuable when the stakes are real. Use it as a genuine thinking
partner, not a checklist of generic objections.

## What this skill does

Given a proposal, plan, decision, argument, or recommendation, the skill:

1. Extracts the key claims and assumptions baked into the idea
2. Challenges each one with the strongest counterargument available
3. Identifies blind spots: things the user has not considered, not just things
   they considered and dismissed
4. Surfaces failure modes: realistic scenarios where this goes wrong
5. Notes what would need to be true for the idea to succeed, and how confident
   you can be that those conditions hold
6. Optionally: steelmans the best version of the opposing view

## How to run the analysis

### Step 1: Extract the core thesis and assumptions

Read the user's input carefully. Identify:
- The central claim or recommendation
- The key assumptions it depends on (explicit and implicit)
- What success looks like and what is being optimized for

If the proposal is ambiguous about its goal or constraints, make your best interpretation
explicit before proceeding.

### Step 2: Challenge the assumptions

For each significant assumption, ask: what if this is wrong? Not in a hand-wavy way,
but specifically. Think about:
- Base rate: is this assumption common or unusual? What do similar cases suggest?
- Reversibility: if this assumption turns out to be wrong, is the decision recoverable?
- Who benefits from this assumption being accepted uncritically? (incentive check)

### Step 3: Find the blind spots

These are different from challenged assumptions, they are things the user has not
mentioned at all. Common categories:
- Stakeholders who are not in the analysis but will be affected
- Dependencies that are not controlled by the user
- Second-order effects (what happens after the first-order result)
- Time horizon mismatches (short-term win, long-term problem)
- What is being given up or deprioritized to pursue this
- Who would actively oppose this, and why

### Step 4: Identify realistic failure modes

Not catastrophic unlikely scenarios, the realistic ones. The ways this kind of
plan typically fails. Be specific rather than generic.

### Step 5: Surface what would need to be true

For the proposal to succeed as described, what has to hold? List the conditions
that are necessary but not guaranteed. Rate each one as:
- Likely: reasonable to assume, most evidence supports it
- Uncertain: could go either way, limited evidence
- Fragile: depends on factors outside the user's control or historically unreliable

### Step 6 (optional): Steelman the opposition

If there is a clear alternative view or opposing position, present the best version
of that argument. This is not the same as validating the opposing view, it is about
understanding it clearly enough to address it properly.

## Output format

Adapt the depth and tone to the stakes. A quick decision warrants a focused response.
A major strategic proposal warrants the full structure.

**Thesis as understood**: [One sentence stating the core claim or plan]

### Hidden Assumptions

**[Assumption 1]**
Status: [Likely / Uncertain / Fragile]
Challenge: [Why this assumption might not hold, with specifics]

**[Assumption 2]**
[Repeat pattern]

[What is missing from the analysis entirely, not just challenged, but unaddressed]

### What Needs to Be True

| Condition | Status | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| [Condition 1] | Likely / Uncertain / Fragile | [one line] |

should know where you actually land.]

## Tone notes

Be honest, not harsh. The goal is to help the user think clearly, not to win an
argument. If an idea is genuinely strong, say so, just be specific about which
parts hold up and which deserve more scrutiny. Avoid vague hedges like "it depends"
without explaining what it depends on and why that matters.
